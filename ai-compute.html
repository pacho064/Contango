AI compute
chokepoints

The same analytical method that maps oil chokepoints applies to AI compute. Where is the stack physically concentrated? Which nodes can be converted into geopolitical leverage? And where does the bottleneck move next?

The central argument: Export controls on AI accelerators target the most visible layer of the compute stack, but the marginal constraint has already migrated — from chips to advanced packaging, then toward HBM memory, and increasingly toward power and grid infrastructure. Policy that tracks the wrong node creates false confidence while the actual bottleneck shifts underneath it.

Chokepoints · August 2026
Where concentration creates leverage

Not all concentration is a chokepoint. A node with 80% market share but 6-month substitution time has limited coercive leverage. A node with 60% concentration but a 5-year qualification cycle may be strategically decisive. The key variables are: substitution difficulty, capacity expansion lead time, and whether a state actor can actually weaponise the concentration.

EUV Lithography
Equipment layer · upstream
CRITICAL
EUV supplier concentration
100%
ASML sole commercial supplier
Geography
Netherlands
+ Carl Zeiss optics (DE) + Trumpf laser (DE)
ASML is the sole commercial supplier of EUV lithography systems required to manufacture chips at sub-3nm nodes — the nodes used in frontier AI accelerators. The concentration is not one building: EUV depends on Carl Zeiss SMT (100% monopoly on EUV optics), TRUMPF (sole EUV laser supplier), and a network of specialist suppliers no other ecosystem has replicated. EUV systems have never been exported to China — controls have held. ASML raised 2026 revenue guidance to €43–45B, up ~19% from prior midpoint, with SK Hynix alone placing a $7B+ multi-year EUV order for HBM expansion.
Why it's a durable chokepoint
Technology monopoly + long replication timeline + Dutch/US allied export control alignment = the most durable control node in the stack. China cannot substitute EUV at commercial yields on any known timeline.
Advanced Packaging (CoWoS)
Assembly layer · current bottleneck
BINDING NOW
CoWoS utilisation
100%
Fully booked · both CoWoS-S and -L
Lead time
50–78 wks
Nvidia holds ~60% of capacity
Advanced packaging is where chips are integrated with HBM into a functional AI accelerator. Taiwan-based companies account for approximately 85% of advanced packaging (CoWoS and InFO). TSMC CoWoS lines are fully booked through 2026–2027, with lead times of 50–78 weeks. Nvidia alone holds ~60% (~595K wafers) of 2026 CoWoS capacity; the top three customers account for >85%. Epoch AI data shows leading AI chip designers accounted for ~90% of global CoWoS and HBM supply in 2025 while using only 12% of advanced logic die production — packaging, not wafer production, became the binding constraint. CoWoS-S costs ~$750/chip; CoWoS-L ~$1,100/chip.
Why it's a durable chokepoint
Manufacturing concentration in Taiwan + 18-month facility lead time + substrate material dependencies (Ajinomoto ABF 95%+ monopoly) + AI rack consumes 10x more ABF than PCs. Capacity is expanding but demand is expanding faster.
High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM)
Memory layer · structural shortage
ALLOCATED 2026–27
SK Hynix market share
~53%
Samsung 35% · Micron 11%
2026 supply status
Sold out
All three vendors · non-cancellable contracts
HBM is the memory architecture required for AI training and inference at scale. The entire global HBM supply from all three producers is allocated under non-cancellable contracts through 2026; 2027 supply is also fully booked. Contract DRAM prices spiked +90–95% QoQ in Q1 2026 — the largest single-quarter spike on record. SK Hynix CEO Kwak Noh-Jung has stated that 2027 will represent the worst supply shortage in the memory industry's history. The chokepoint is not a single supplier monopoly but extreme three-player concentration with ~18-24 month capacity expansion timelines, co-qualification requirements with Nvidia/AMD/Google, and HBM4E yield risks (12-stack to 8-stack downgrade under evaluation for Rubin Ultra).
Why it's a durable chokepoint
South Korea controls ~88% of HBM through SK Hynix + Samsung. Micron (US) holds 11%. China's CXMT is targeting HBM3 yields in 2026 — progress is real but still 2–3 generations behind at commercial scale.

TSMC controls approximately 90% of advanced logic chip production below 7nm. Taiwan-based companies account for ~67% of total foundry output. TSMC's 2nm (N2) node is fully booked well into 2028; new 3nm project kickoffs have been suspended with customers steered toward 2nm. TSMC has committed $265B in total US manufacturing investment, with 2026 CapEx raised to $60–64B. TSMC's Arizona fab covers less than 5% of Taiwan capacity — reshoring is real but the timeline is 2027–2028 for meaningful advanced logic output, and packaging/yield ecosystems will lag further.
Why it's a durable chokepoint
Taiwan geopolitical risk = systemic AI hardware risk. Arizona diversification is happening but insufficient before 2028. Samsung 2nm and Intel 18A are ramping with limited external capacity — real alternatives but at 12–18 month qualification cycles.
EDA software & IP
Design layer · ecosystem lock-in
ECOSYSTEM LOCK
US EDA market share
>90%
Synopsys · Cadence · Mentor
Arm CPU architecture
UK/US
Licenses to virtually all chip designers
Electronic Design Automation software — the tools required to design chips — is dominated by US firms (Synopsys, Cadence, Mentor/Siemens EDA). This creates a different form of chokepoint: not physical scarcity but ecosystem lock-in. Switching EDA tools requires redesigning entire chip development flows, retooling verification and simulation, and rebuilding process design kit integrations — measured in years, not months. BIS export controls now cover EDA for advanced node chip design in China. Arm's architecture license underpins nearly all mobile and many data center chips globally, with Arm itself subject to US jurisdiction.
Why it's different from physical chokepoints
Not scarcity but switching cost. China is building domestic EDA (Primarius, Empyrean) and RISC-V alternatives to Arm — making progress at mature nodes but not yet at advanced nodes.
Power & grid infrastructure
Infrastructure layer · emerging constraint
EMERGING
US data center share of electricity
9.5–15%
projected 2030 · Berkeley Lab
Grid transformer lead time
1–2+ yrs

CSIS has explicitly identified electricity supply as a binding constraint on AI expansion. The bottleneck is not total generation capacity but grid interconnection, transformer procurement, and permitting timelines — infrastructure constraints that do not respond to capital the way chip fabs do. Berkeley Lab estimates US data centers could reach 9.5–15.3% of US electricity use by 2030. This creates a geographic dimension: AI compute gravitates toward electricity-abundant regions, shifting relative advantages in ways that are decoupled from semiconductor geography. Nuclear power restarts, long-duration storage, and grid interconnection reform are now AI policy issues.
Why this matters for the next 3 years
If chip supply expands (as CoWoS and HBM capacity ramps), the bottleneck migrates to power. The constraint that limits frontier AI training in 2028 may not be silicon — it may be megawatts.

Four forms of leverage
Not all chokepoints are the same kind of power

A market-share chokepoint and a legal-jurisdictional chokepoint work through completely different mechanisms. Conflating them leads to policy errors — controlling the wrong node, or expecting market leverage to function like legal leverage.

Technology monopoly
ASML · EUV
Sole commercial EUV supplier · Netherlands
No country can manufacture frontier AI chips without EUV lithography equipment, and only ASML makes it commercially. The leverage is exercised through Dutch and EU export licensing policy — ASML cannot independently deny or grant access; the Dutch state controls that decision. This is the cleanest monopoly in the stack, but the coercive instrument is the state, not the firm.
Manufacturing concentration
TSMC · leading-edge foundry
~90% advanced logic · Taiwan
TSMC has market power — not geopolitical leverage it can independently deploy. Taiwan does not use TSMC as a coercive foreign policy instrument. The strategic importance of TSMC is primarily about Taiwan's vulnerability rather than Taiwan's leverage: a military contingency that disrupts TSMC disrupts every AI hardware program in the world simultaneously.
Supplier concentration
SK Hynix · Samsung · HBM
South Korea 88% HBM · US 11% (Micron)
South Korea's HBM concentration creates strategic relevance for US-Korea alliance dynamics — not direct coercive power. If Korean HBM producers were subject to export restrictions (as China-facing HBM controls demonstrate is possible), AI hardware supply would be severely disrupted. The leverage is structural, not actively wielded. South Korea's position is closer to a potential target of leverage than a source of it.
Jurisdictional leverage
US BIS · export controls
Bureau of Industry and Security · Commerce Dept
BIS possesses actual legal coercive capacity through the Export Administration Regulations and the foreign-produced direct product rule, which extends US jurisdiction to any chip made anywhere using US technology, software, or equipment — which is nearly all of them. This is categorically different from market concentration. It is jurisdictional reach that does not require the US to hold manufacturing capacity itself.

Export control timeline · 2022–2026
How controls moved from chips to the production stack

The trajectory of US semiconductor export controls shows a deliberate shift: from restricting access to specific chips toward restricting China's ability to produce advanced chips domestically. Each rule change expanded the control perimeter further upstream in the stack.

Oct 2022
PERIMETER SHIFT
BIS imposes sweeping chip and equipment controls on China — largest restriction in a generation
What changed: from controlling specific exports → constraining China's ability to produce advanced chips domestically. Controls covered advanced computing chips, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and US-person support for advanced PRC fabs. The foreign-produced direct product rule was substantially expanded.
BIS Federal Register · October 7 2022
Oct 2023
LOOPHOLE CLOSED
Updated rules close performance threshold loopholes — anti-circumvention provisions strengthened
What changed: chip-performance thresholds revised to prevent near-controlled chips from serving as substitutes. Country-level controls expanded. Third-country enforcement of FDPR strengthened to prevent circumvention through intermediaries.
BIS Federal Register · October 17 2023
Jan 2025
GLOBAL SCOPE
AI Diffusion Rule — first attempt to regulate global access to advanced AI chips and model weights
What changed: controls attempted to tier global access to AI compute across three country categories. Covered advanced AI chips and certain model weights. Represented a significant conceptual shift: from bilateral US-China controls toward multilateral global AI governance through export control architecture.
BIS Federal Register · January 13 2025 · CSIS Wadhwani analysis
Jan 2026
PARTIAL REOPENING
H200 licensing framework — case-by-case review for approved Chinese customers introduced
What changed: BIS moved from blanket H200 restriction to a licensed-export regime for approved Chinese entities. Conditions attached; policy certainty limited to 12-month windows. Created a managed restriction rather than hard decoupling — preserving limited revenue channels for US firms while maintaining strategic control.
BIS · Commerce Dept · January 2026
Mar 2026
CAPACITY REDIRECT
Nvidia redirects TSMC CoWoS capacity from H200 to Vera Rubin — export uncertainty reshapes production planning
What changed: regulatory uncertainty over H200 China sales caused Nvidia to reallocate constrained CoWoS packaging capacity toward next-generation Vera Rubin chips with confirmed US/allied orders. Illustrates how export control uncertainty, not just enforcement, shapes supply allocation decisions. Vera Rubin entered full production May 2026.
Financial Times · March 2026 · Nvidia Newsroom May 2026 · CNAS May 2026
2026 ongoing
CHINA COUNTER
China accelerates domestic stack — Huawei Ascend, CXMT HBM, SMIC 7nm, domestic EDA
What changed: China's substitution strategy is producing real progress at mature nodes while remaining constrained at the frontier. The policy question is not whether China can substitute everything — it is which specific nodes remain durable chokepoints as substitution improves, and what the economic penalty of substitution is relative to the frontier.

Where Beijing can substitute — and at what cost

The right question is not whether China is "catching up" as a single score. It is: which specific nodes remain genuine chokepoints, which are closing, and what is the economic penalty of substitution relative to the frontier? A technically viable substitute that costs 3× more and yields 40% less is not a commercially viable substitute at scale.

The substitution cost question
China's substitution strategy does not need to match the frontier to be strategically significant — it needs to be good enough to sustain AI development at sufficient scale and acceptable cost. The policy question is: at what point does the economic penalty of substitution become tolerable for China's AI development goals, and which specific chokepoints remain constraining after that threshold is crossed? That question has a different answer for every node in the stack.

Where the constraint migrates next
The bottleneck is not fixed

The most important analytical question in AI compute is not which node is constrained today — it is where the constraint migrates as investment responds. CoWoS was not the bottleneck in 2022. It became the bottleneck in 2025 when chip supply expanded and packaging could not keep up. The same dynamic is already visible in power and grid infrastructure.

Training vs inference distinction matters here. Frontier model training and large-scale inference have different hardware, memory, power, and geographic requirements. A chokepoint critical for training a 1-trillion-parameter model may matter much less for serving 500 million inference requests daily — and vice versa. "AI compute" is not one market.

Current signals suggest the next migration: as TSMC, SK Hynix, and Micron expand CoWoS and HBM capacity through 2027–2028, and as the AI hardware market fragments between frontier training, inference, and edge deployment, the binding constraint may shift toward power availability, grid interconnection lead times, and cooling infrastructure — constraints that do not respond to capital the way chip fabs do, and that are geographically distributed rather than concentrated in a few countries.

If that migration occurs, the policy implications are significant: the most important AI industrial policy decisions in 2028 may be about permitting reform, transmission infrastructure, and nuclear power — not chip export controls.
